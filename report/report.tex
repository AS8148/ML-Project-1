\title{Predicting Coronary Heart Disease from Survey Answers\\
with Gradient Boosting in NumPy}

\author{
  Samuel Bachmann, Luca Civilla, Andrew Siminszky\\
  \textit{CS-433 Machine Learning, EPFL, Switzerland}
}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
  We predict coronary heart disease (MICHD) from the answers of 328,135 people to
  the BRFSS 2015 survey, scored by the F1 of the positive class (8.8\% of the
  people). We decode the answer codes of the survey, compare the six methods of
  the course on the same features, and implement gradient boosted trees in NumPy.
  Every model predicts ``positive'' above a threshold chosen to maximize F1, not
  above 0.5. Our submitted model, boosting on 260 decoded columns, reaches an F1 of
  $0.444 \pm 0.004$ in 5-fold cross-validation and 0.454 on AIcrowd, against
  0.438 and 0.447 for the regularized logistic regression. A heavier blend of
  boosted trees and neural networks gives our team's best AIcrowd score, 0.456.
  One question, asked only after a heart attack, gives away the label of 0.24\%
  of the people; we use it and measure its effect.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The 321 columns of the data are answers, variables computed by the CDC from the
answers, and fields of the survey administration~\cite{brfss15}. Since only one
person in eleven is positive, predicting ``no'' for everyone gives an accuracy of
0.912 but finds nobody, which is why the challenge scores the F1 of the
positive class.

\section{Data, Features and Validation}\label{sec:data}

\paragraph{Decoding}
The values are survey codes, not numbers: in \texttt{PHYSHLTH} (``days of poor
physical health'') 88 means none, 77 ``don't know'' and 99 ``refused''. We map
``don't know'', ``refused'' and ``not asked'' codes to missing values and ``none''
codes to 0, convert answers coded with a unit (``203'' means 3 times per week),
and re-number answers whose codes have no meaningful order (high blood pressure:
1 yes, 3 no, 4 borderline become 2, 0, 1). Many questions are asked only after a
given answer (skip patterns), so whether a value is missing carries information.

\paragraph{Two inputs}
The course methods use 96 questions selected by hand (43 secondary ones, e.g.\ on
diet, vaccines or diabetes care), decoded by rules written for them: 11 nominal
answers are one-hot encoded with ``missing'' as a level (levels with fewer than
200 rows dropped, the most frequent one as reference), numeric answers are
median-imputed with a missing indicator when at least 10\% are missing, and all
features are standardized and clipped to $\pm8$ ($\approx$220 features). The
gradient boosting reads 260 columns instead: all but 61 administrative (dates,
sampling weights, phone screening) and redundant ones, decoded with tables
written from the codebook of the provided data~\cite{brfss15}; missing values get their own bin. All statistics are
estimated on the training rows of each fold.

\paragraph{The HAREHAB1 answer}
The question HAREHAB1 (rehabilitation after a heart attack) is asked only of
people who reported a heart attack, in four states. It is answered in 773
training rows, all positive, and in 238 test rows. It is in the provided data,
so we use it (as an ``answered'' flag for the linear models, a bin for the
trees), but a real screening could not: without it the logistic regression loses
0.87 percentage points (pp) of F1 (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\paragraph{Validation and threshold}
We use stratified 5-fold cross-validation (fold seed 42). In each fold everything
is fitted on the four training folds, including the decision threshold that
maximizes F1, and the F1 is measured on the fifth fold. To compare two models
we subtract their F1 fold by fold, in pp (1~pp $=0.01$ of F1). Before tuning
the boosting we fixed a rule: a change is kept only if it gains at least 0.30 pp and
is positive on at least 4 of 5 folds, both on these folds and on a second
split into 5 folds (seed 43).

The six functions of \texttt{implementations.py} follow the course conventions:
the squared loss has a factor $1/2$, the logistic losses use labels in $\{0,1\}$,
and the regularized losses are returned without their penalty. The regularized
logistic regression minimizes the summed log-loss plus $(\lambda_C/2)\|w\|^2$
with $\lambda_C=10$, that is $\lambda = \lambda_C/(2N) \approx 1.9\cdot10^{-5}$
in the convention of the course ($N\approx2.6\cdot10^5$ training rows). The
threshold of these models maximizes F1 on their training predictions.

Table~\ref{tab:methods} shows that the loss matters more than the optimizer: the
four squared-loss methods reach 0.421--0.424, the two logistic regressions 0.438.
The penalty barely matters ($\lambda_C$ from 1 to 100 moves F1 by at most
0.03 pp), so ridge keeps a small $\lambda$ and equals least squares.
GD (step $0.9/L$, below the divergence limit $2/L$) and SGD, stopped after a
fixed number of steps, stay slightly below. Gradient descent on the logistic
loss matches Newton's method within 0.01~pp. In the upper block of
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, decoding the answers, the HAREHAB1 answer and the
secondary questions all help; the state and the missing indicators stay within
the noise of the folds.

\section{Part 2: Gradient Boosting}\label{sec:part2}

\paragraph{Model}
Gradient boosting~\cite{friedman01} adds trees that each fit the gradient
$g=p-y$ and the curvature $h=p(1-p)$ of the log-loss at the current predictions
$p$. Each column is cut into at most 32 quantile bins plus a bin for missing
values. Trees have depth 3;
each split also chooses the side of the missing values and keeps a sum of $h$ of
at least 5 per side; a leaf predicts $-\sum g/(\sum h + 10)$, an $L_2$
penalty on the leaf values. Each tree sees a random half of the rows and of
the columns and is shrunk by a rate of 0.05. The trees are fitted on 80\% of the
training rows; the other 20\% stop the boosting when their log-loss has not
improved for 30 trees (at most 1000) and set the threshold. On the full training
set this gives 896 trees and a threshold of probability 0.20.

\paragraph{Results}
On the same 96 questions, boosting adds 0.40~pp to the logistic regression,
positive on all 5 folds (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). The 260 codebook columns add
0.16 pp on seed 42 (2 of 5 folds positive) and 0.35 pp on seed 43 (5 of 5), so
they fail our rule on the first split. We kept them as an exception, decided
after also seeing the AIcrowd score: over all 10 folds the gain is +0.26 pp
(positive on 7), twice that of averaging with a neural network (64 ReLU units,
+0.13~pp), and only the input changes, not the model. The other variants of the
lower block did not pass the rule. Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold} shows the largest
single lever: the default threshold 0.5 flags too few people and costs 19 pp,
while the threshold chosen on the validation rows ($p \approx 0.2$, about 13\%
flagged) is close to the best one of every fold.

\paragraph{Team blend}
Our team's best AIcrowd score, 0.456, comes from a heavier model: boosted trees of depth 6 (300 trees, 4 seeds, weight 0.7) averaged
with neural networks with two hidden layers (10 seeds, weight 0.3). Its
cross-validated F1, 0.446, uses other folds and a single
threshold chosen on the same out-of-fold predictions it is scored on, so it is
slightly optimistic. It gains 0.2 pp on AIcrowd but runs for about an hour, so we
submitted the simpler model.

\section{Discussion}
Once the data are decoded, the logistic regression and the boosting differ by
less than 0.7 pp, and the gains of the boosting and of the codebook columns
are of the order of the fold-to-fold noise (standard error about
0.2 pp~\cite{nadeau03}). The leaderboard moved with the cross-validation (0.447,
0.452, 0.454) but sits about 0.9 pp higher, and with about 9,700 expected
positives its own standard error is about 0.4 pp. Precision stays low (0.38 in
cross-validation, at a recall of 0.54): many sick people give the same
self-reported answers as healthy ones, and part of what the model ``predicts''
is a heart attack that people already reported, through HAREHAB1.

\paragraph{Reproducibility}
\texttt{run.py} reproduces our submitted file (8.5 minutes once the data are
cached), \texttt{experiments.py} the cross-validation results (except five rejected
variants, see the README) and Fig.~\ref{fig:threshold}, and
\texttt{blend/run\_blend.py} the blend (about one hour).
